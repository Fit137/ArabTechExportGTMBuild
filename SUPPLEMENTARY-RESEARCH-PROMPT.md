# Supplementary research prompt, for Reflexion

Copy everything between the rules below and paste it as one prompt. It is
self-contained, the research tool needs no access to this repo.

If the tool caps scope per run, run **Block 1 alone first**. It is the only block
that gates the product itself rather than the marketing, and blocks 2 to 9 are
still useful if block 1 is all you get through.

---

You are conducting supplementary market and operational research for a pre-launch
business. A first research pass has already been done. Your job is to close the
gaps it left and to verify two findings it holds with low confidence.

## The business

**Arab Tech Export** (arabtechexport.com, site not yet live) teaches Arab
technology builders and companies to sell AI products and services into the United
States and Canada. Two offers, two buyers, same subject.

- **Track B, individuals.** Arab software engineers, indie hackers, AI consultants
  and AI service providers. Delivered through a paid community, courses, one to one
  sessions and AMA sessions.
- **Track A, companies.** Arab technology companies with an established product,
  entering the United States and Canada. Delivered through sales team training and
  market entry strategy.

It is pre-launch: no customers, no revenue, no results, no testimonials.

**Explicitly not offered, and not to be researched as if it were:** leads,
introductions, a buyer network, placement, recruitment, visa or relocation support,
or selling on a client's behalf. The business teaches and advises.

**Working assumption on markets, which block 7 should test:** Egypt, Jordan,
Morocco, Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates.

## What the first pass already established. Do not re-research these

Stated so you do not spend effort re-deriving them. Correct any that are wrong, but
do not treat them as open questions.

- Competitor set for Track B: Hsoub Academy (academy.hsoub.com), The Futur
  (thefutur.com), Upwork, Mostaql (mostaql.com), Egypt FWD (egfwd.com).
- Competitor set for Track A: Sandler (Dubai franchise active), hiring a target
  market sales leader, outsourced sales agencies, ITIDA Export IT (Egypt),
  Big Four and MBB firms.
- ITIDA Export IT: launched 2010, 15 rounds, 370 plus companies supported,
  incentives of 35 percent micro, 15 percent small, 10 percent medium.
- Paid community churn: 30 to 50 percent in the first 14 days on poorly onboarded
  cohorts. Monthly churn of 8 to 10 percent reads as an onboarding failure.
- Egypt card constraint: monthly USD card caps reported as low as $50 to $200,
  international limits reported suspended on debit cards, FX fees cut from 5 to
  3 percent at NBE, CIB and Banque Misr.
- United States VP Sales benchmarks: median SaaS base about $157,500, Series B OTE
  $360,000 to $425,000, about 106 days average time to fill, 3 to 6 months ramp.
- United States AI consulting rates: solo $150 to $350 per hour, boutique agency
  $250 to $450, mid tier firms $300 to $600, Big Four $400 to $800, MBB $500 to
  $1,000 plus. Small project $10,000 to $40,000, medium $40,000 to $150,000.

## How to report, and what disqualifies an answer

1. **Every claim carries a working URL.** No claim without a source. Prefer primary
   sources: the company's own page, the regulator, the government programme, the
   platform's own documentation. Review aggregators and listicles are last resort
   and must be labelled as such.
2. **Mark every finding with a confidence level.**
   - **V, verified.** Primary source, or two independent sources agreeing.
   - **P, partial.** Confirmed but incomplete, or from a secondary source only.
   - **T, thin.** The question was asked properly and nothing verifiable came back.
3. **Record thin as thin. Do not fill a gap with a plausible answer.** "No named
   entity found after checking X, Y and Z" is a valuable, usable result. An invented
   one poisons every downstream decision. This matters most in blocks 3 and 4, where
   the first pass already came back thin and the temptation to produce something is
   highest.
4. **Date stamp anything volatile.** Prices, card limits, tax rates, programme
   rounds and platform availability all move. Give the date the source was published
   or last updated.
5. **Do not substitute a software review site.** This category is not on G2 or
   Capterra. If a search returns an unrelated software company with a similar name,
   discard it rather than reporting it.
6. **Distinguish what buyers say from what vendors claim.** A vendor's own case
   study is evidence of positioning, not of outcome.
7. **No income or earnings claims.** Do not report, and do not construct, any figure
   for what a trainee, member, freelancer or client earned or could earn. Published
   market rate benchmarks for third parties are fine and are useful. A projection of
   what someone would make after buying something is not, and will be discarded.

# BLOCK 1. What a North American buyer actually checks. Highest priority

**Why this is first.** The entire business rests on the claim that it teaches what a
United States or Canada buyer verifies before awarding work to a foreign vendor. The
first pass established that no competitor packages this. It did not establish what
the checks actually are. This block is the curriculum, not the marketing. Without it
there is a positioning with nothing behind it.

Research what a B2B buyer in the United States and Canada actually requires before
contracting a software or AI services vendor based outside North America.

### 1.1 The vendor onboarding and procurement path

- The real sequence from first contact to signed contract and first invoice paid.
- Which steps are gated by whom: the economic buyer, legal, procurement, security,
  finance, and where a foreign vendor stalls that a domestic one does not.
- Dollar thresholds that trigger legal review, security review, or competitive
  bidding, and how those thresholds differ by company size.
- Typical elapsed time per stage.
- How this differs across three buyer sizes: startup under 50 people, mid market
  50 to 1,000, enterprise over 1,000.
- How Canada differs from the United States.

### 1.2 Tax, legal and contracting requirements specific to a foreign vendor

- **W-8BEN and W-8BEN-E.** When each is required, what a foreign vendor must supply,
  what happens without one, and the withholding consequence.
- Whether an EIN or ITIN is needed, and in what circumstances.
- Whether a United States entity is genuinely required, or only commonly assumed to
  be. What a foreign vendor can and cannot do without one.
- Standard contracting artifacts: MSA, SOW, NDA, DPA, and what a buyer expects each
  to contain.
- Clauses that recur and that foreign vendors commonly get wrong: IP assignment,
  work made for hire, governing law and venue, limitation of liability, indemnity,
  non-solicit.
- Payment terms norms: net 30, net 45, net 60, and how a foreign vendor is actually
  paid. ACH, wire, platform, and who bears the fees.
- Invoicing requirements that cause rejection.

### 1.3 Security, compliance and insurance

- When SOC 2 Type II is actually required against when it is merely asked for, and
  what substitutes are accepted from a small vendor.
- Security questionnaires in common use, and what a small foreign vendor realistically
  answers.
- Data residency and data transfer expectations, including GDPR and CCPA or CPRA
  where relevant, and what a DPA has to cover.
- Insurance: professional liability or errors and omissions, cyber liability, general
  liability. Typical minimum coverage amounts demanded in contracts, and whether a
  foreign vendor can obtain policies a United States buyer will accept.
- Background check and identity verification expectations.
- **Sanctions and export control screening.** Whether and how United States buyers
  screen foreign vendors, what OFAC screening covers in practice, and whether vendors
  in Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Saudi Arabia or the United Arab Emirates encounter
  friction from it. Report what is documented, not what is assumed. This is a
  legitimate operational question and the answer may well be that these five markets
  present no issue.

### 1.4 The soft checks

- What signals a buyer reads before the formal process starts: web presence,
  references, case studies, the first written reply, how a proposal is structured.
- What causes a buyer to stop replying after a good first call, from buyer side
  accounts rather than vendor side speculation.
- Whether and how time zone, language and location are raised, and whether buyers
  say so openly or only privately.

### 1.5 Deliverable for this block

A consolidated checklist of what a North American buyer verifies, ordered by where it
occurs in the buying process, each item marked as: required by most buyers, required
by some, or nice to have. Plus a separate short list of the items most commonly
missing from a foreign vendor's setup.

# BLOCK 3. Named competitors the first pass could not find

Two searches came back empty. Empty after one pass is weak evidence. Run them harder,
and if they are still empty, say so with the search paths you took.

### 3.1 Outbound market entry specialists, MENA to North America

- Consultancies, boutiques, advisory firms or individual advisors who take Middle
  East or North Africa technology companies **into** the United States or Canada.
  Note that most search results run the opposite direction, taking international
  companies into the Gulf. Those are not what is being asked for.

- Also look from the receiving side: soft landing programmes, landing pads, and
  market entry support run by or for the destination. SelectUSA, state economic
  development organisations, Canadian Trade Commissioner Service, MaRS, Communitech,

### 3.2 Paid programmes teaching technical people to sell to Western clients

  Latin America and South East Asia, since the model may exist there and not yet in
  the Arab region.
- For each: name, URL, price, format, cohort or always-open, audience size if
  disclosed, and what they promise and explicitly refuse to promise.
- This is the closest available evidence of what works in this category, and the
  first pass did not look outside the Arab region for it.

# BLOCK 5. Payment rails, both directions. High priority

The first pass evidenced Egypt and Morocco. Extend it, and add the collection side,
which was not researched at all and which constrains how the business can be priced.

### 5.1 Can the customer pay us

For each of Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates:

- Current limits on international or cross-border card spending for individuals.
  Monthly caps, debit against credit differences, whether limits are suspended, and
  the date of the information.
- Foreign exchange fees and any surcharge on international transactions.
- Whether a recurring monthly USD subscription charge reliably clears.
- Local alternatives that do clear: bank transfer, local wallets, instalment
  providers, local card schemes such as mada, local gateways such as Fawry, Paymob,
  PayTabs, Tap, HyperPay, Moyasar, Telr.
- Any regulatory limit on individuals purchasing foreign digital services.

### 5.2 Can we collect the money

- Which merchant of record and payment platforms can sell digital products and
  subscriptions **into** these five markets, and which accept a seller entity
  domiciled in each of: a Gulf state, Egypt, the United Kingdom, the United States,
  and the European Union. Cover at least Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, Stripe, Gumroad,
  Chargebee, 2Checkout or Verifone, and the regional gateways above.
- Which platforms support local payment methods in each market, not just cards.
- **VAT and tax on digital services sold into each of the five markets:** rate,
  registration threshold, whether a foreign seller must register, and whether a
  merchant of record removes that obligation. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab
  Emirates in particular.
- Practical consequence: state whether a single global monthly USD subscription is
  viable across all five markets, or whether annual, cohort or locally collected
  billing is necessary. This is the question the block exists to answer.

### 5.3 Can the member get paid, which is also the product

- Current availability, limits and restrictions for Payoneer, Wise, Deel, Remote,
  PayPal and direct bank wire, for an individual in each of the five markets
  receiving payment from a United States or Canada client.
- Any requirement to repatriate or convert foreign currency earnings.
- Tax registration or declaration obligations for a freelancer receiving foreign
  income in each market.

# BLOCK 7. The origin markets, tested rather than assumed

The five market list is a working assumption. Test it and replace it with evidence.

- Estimated population of software developers, and separately of AI or machine
  learning practitioners, in each of Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Saudi Arabia, the United
  Arab Emirates, plus Tunisia, Algeria, Lebanon and the remaining Gulf states.
- Estimated numbers working as freelancers or independent service providers, and the

- English proficiency by market, using a published comparative index, and any

- Which markets produce the most technology services exports, and where the outbound
  activity actually concentrates.
- Where an Arab technical audience consumes professional and technical content, and
  in which language. Platform data rather than opinion. Include the split between
  Arabic and English for technical content specifically, since this decides the
  language of every marketing asset.
- Named online communities where this audience already gathers: Discord and Slack
  servers, Telegram and WhatsApp groups, subreddits, forums, and recurring events or
  meetups. Name them and give joining routes where public.
- Diaspora: whether Arab technical diaspora in North America and Europe form a
  distinct segment, and whether they consume Arabic content.

# BLOCK 9. Cross-border engagement mechanics, for the company track

Track A sells consulting to companies in these markets. The first pass did not
research what that actually involves.

- **Withholding tax** on payments for services made by a company in each of the five
  markets to a foreign consultant or training provider. Rates, and whether a double
  taxation treaty reduces them.
- Whether central bank approval, foreign supplier registration or currency control
  clearance is required before a company can pay a foreign invoice, in each market.
  Egypt in particular.
- Typical procurement route and approval thresholds for a mid-sized private company
  buying consulting in each market.
- Whether invoicing must be in local currency, and any e-invoicing mandate that
  affects a foreign supplier. Saudi Arabia and Egypt both have e-invoicing regimes.
- **Validate the hire comparison.** The first pass used United States SaaS VP Sales
  benchmarks. Check whether that is the right comparator for an Arab technology
  company's first North American sales hire, and research the alternatives it would
  actually consider: a fractional or part-time sales leader and their market rates, an
  employer of record arrangement through Deel, Remote or similar and its all-in cost
  for a United States sales hire made by a foreign company, and commission-only or
  agency-based representation.

# BLOCK 11. Insurance obtainability in the origin markets. Added after Block 1

Block 1 established that North American buyers commonly require general liability at
USD 1,000,000 per occurrence or above, plus professional liability or errors and
omissions, and cyber liability, with a certificate of insurance requested during
onboarding. It also found that documented insurance at expected limits is one of the
four things most commonly missing from a foreign vendor's setup.

What it did not establish is whether a vendor in the origin markets can obtain those
policies at all. That question decides whether this is teachable curriculum or a dead
end, so it is worth a block of its own.

For each of Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, and for
two vendor shapes, a solo individual and a company of five to fifty people:

- Which insurers write professional liability or errors and omissions, cyber
  liability, and commercial general liability for technology and software services
  providers. Name them with URLs.
- Typical coverage limits available, and whether USD 1,000,000 per occurrence is
  routinely achievable.
- Indicative premium ranges, if published.
- **Whether the policy would be accepted by a North American buyer.** This is the
  crux. Does the certificate need to be issued by an insurer licensed or admitted in
  the United States or Canada, does it need to name the buyer as an additional
  insured, does it need to be denominated in USD, and will a policy written locally in
  one of these five markets satisfy a standard vendor onboarding requirement.
- Whether international brokers write cross-border policies for vendors in these
  markets, and whether a vendor without a North American entity can be covered.
- Whether any of the government export or ICT programmes already identified in Block 8
  subsidise or arrange such coverage. Export credit agencies in particular.
- Whether contracting through a platform, an employer of record, or an agency of
  record transfers the insurance requirement away from the individual vendor, and at
  what cost.

**Report a clear answer to one question above all:** can a competent solo builder in
each of these five markets realistically satisfy a North American buyer's insurance
requirement, and if so by what route. If the answer is no in some markets, say which,
because that changes what deals those members can win and belongs in the customer
profile rather than in the curriculum.

# Output format

Return the blocks in order, each as its own section, using this shape per finding:

| Finding | Detail | Confidence | Date of source | URL |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Then, at the end, three short sections:

1. **What changed.** Anything you found that contradicts the first pass findings
   listed near the top of this prompt. Flag each one explicitly, since downstream work
   is built on them.
2. **Still thin.** Every question that came back without a verifiable answer, with the
   search paths you tried, so the next pass does not repeat them.
3. **The five things that most change a decision.** Your judgement, with reasoning.
   Not a summary of everything, a ranking of what matters.
